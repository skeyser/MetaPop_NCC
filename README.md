## Abstract
Global rewilding initiatives continue to repatriate carnivores to locations across their historic ranges, but these efforts are occurring in regions that are experiencing rapid climate change. The reintroduction of species to changing environments requires understanding the current demographic context, connectivity between reintroduced populations, and future conditions under expected change. The American marten is the most reintroduced carnivore in North America and rewilding efforts in the Great Lakes region have successfully established a recovery network. However, given their reliance on reliable snowpack, the viability of this metapopulation is uncertain under future warming. Using >1,200 individually genotyped and geolocated martens across the upper Great Lakes Region, we developed a spatially explicit population viability analysis to assess the future regional viability of marten in response to shifting winter conditions. Marten demographics were sensitive to regional declines in snow depth, leading to declines in abundance and occupancy across the metapopulation, despite marked variability across six analyzed regional climate model simulations. Connectivity stabilized regional patch occupancy in contemporary and mid-century climate conditions but could not overcome late-century population declines. Critically, connectivity between populations played a secondary role compared to magnitude of future winter climate scenarios. Patch-level extinction probability was largely mediated by patch size, with the largest habitat patch sizes expected to persist 100 years into the future. We demonstrate that future climate change can disrupt and even overwhelm contemporary rescue effects of metapopulations, eroding the future persistence of rewilded species, but that the largest reintroduction areas will have the greatest chance of persistence into the future underscoring the importance of preserving and rewilding large areas of habitat.
